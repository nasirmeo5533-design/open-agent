Meta Ads vs Google Ads: How to Choose for Your Business

By Abeer Nasir · OpenAgent

Meta Ads and Google Ads do different jobs. For a business with a limited budget, the choice depends on where your buyers already look when they decide.

Meta Ads: demand creation

Meta (Facebook and Instagram) is where you show your offer to people who were not looking for it yet. It is a strong fit for visual offers because the creative does the explaining. You reach cold audiences, give them a reason to care, and stay in front of people who already engaged.

Best for: new offers, launches, retargeting and anything that needs to be seen to be understood. A small-budget Meta Ads test typically runs 7–14 days: lead with short-form video, keep what works, and pause what does not.

Google Ads: demand capture

Google reaches people who are already searching for a product, service or solution. They have intent; your job is to be relevant when that search happens. Search ads work best when the offer and landing experience match the query.

Best for: proven offers, high-intent keywords, and defending your brand name from competitors bidding on it.

Which one first?

  • New offer, no search volume yet → start with Meta Ads to create demand.
  • People already searching for you → add Google Ads to capture that intent.
  • Small budget → pick one, measure it properly, then expand.

Is there a universal budget split?

A question owners ask later: "I have both now, so how should I split spend?" The honest answer: there is no universal ratio. Start where demand currently lives. For conversation-led offers that is usually Meta; for high-intent search it is often Google. Then let each month's numbers, not a fixed rule, argue for any shift. Cost per qualified lead is compared in every report, and if the balance needs adjusting, that adjustment is named and explained. A split set once and never reviewed is just a comfortable guess.

When search is competitive

In competitive markets, search auctions get expensive, but high-intent search often converts well because the buyer is already looking. Many buyers also check reviews and a clear landing page before they enquire, so the page your ad sends them to matters as much as the ad itself. A useful guiding question is simple: where does my buyer already look when they decide? That question usually decides the first channel.

Consent rules and tracking

In the EU and the UK, consent rules shape tracking before the first ad runs. Since March 2024 Google expects advertisers to send consent signals through Consent Mode v2 for visitors in the EEA and the UK, and without them remarketing and conversion measurement get much weaker. The Meta pixel should also wait for a cookie-banner yes under GDPR and ePrivacy rules. Set up consent first, or both platforms will optimise on thin data and report fewer conversions than you actually get.

A simple starting rule

Use Meta to create interest and Google to catch active searches. Start with one channel, track qualified leads and sales, and add the second when the first is working.

Where creative and social fit

Good ads need a steady supply of useful images and short-form video, and the profile people land on still matters. If you are building that supply, the social media content service covers asset production, while social media management keeps the organic presence consistent alongside paid traffic.

How to decide in practice

It is rarely an either/or. Most accounts I plan end with both channels working different jobs: Meta for the top of the funnel, Google for the people already shopping. If you are starting from zero, pick the channel that matches where the demand lives today. If nobody is searching for you yet, run Meta first.
