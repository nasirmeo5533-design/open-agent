Free Trial or Demo? How to Choose the Right SaaS Funnel

By the OpenAgent team

Every SaaS website has to answer one question on its main button: what should a visitor do next? "Start free trial", "Get started free" and "Book a demo" lead to very different funnels, sales teams and marketing budgets. The free trial vs demo decision shapes how you acquire, convert and retain customers, so it is worth making deliberately rather than copying a competitor.

This guide explains when each model fits, how to decide for your product, and how the choice changes your marketing.

Short answer: use a free trial or freemium when buyers can understand the product and reach value on their own in a short time, and the price suits self-serve purchase. Use a demo when the product is complex, needs setup or data, involves several decision-makers, or sells at a price that justifies a sales conversation. Many SaaS companies run a hybrid: self-serve for small teams, demos for larger accounts.

The three main SaaS funnel models

Free trial

Full or near-full access for a limited period, usually 7 to 30 days. Some ask for a card upfront, others don't. The trial's job is to get the user to a clear moment of value before it ends.

Freemium

A permanently free plan with limits on features, usage or seats, and paid plans for more. Freemium works when free users create value (word of mouth, network effects, content) and some naturally grow into paying.

Demo-led (sales-led)

The visitor books a call, a salesperson qualifies them and shows the product, then the deal moves through a sales process. Often paired with a guided trial or pilot after the demo.

How to choose: five questions

1. How quickly can a user reach value alone?

If a new user can sign up and get a useful result in minutes (send an invoice, schedule a post, build a form), a trial or freemium fits. If value appears only after integrations, data imports or configuration, an unguided trial usually ends before the user gets there.

2. What does the product cost?

Lower monthly prices rarely justify a salesperson's time per customer. Higher annual contracts usually need sales involvement, security reviews and procurement. There is no fixed cutoff, but the cost of acquiring a customer has to fit the revenue they bring.

3. Who buys it?

A freelancer or small team can decide alone, today, with a card. A company buying for hundreds of employees involves several people: the user, a manager, IT, finance. Demos and sales processes exist to handle that group.

4. How different is your product?

If buyers understand the category ("project management tool"), they can evaluate a trial on their own. If you are creating a new category or the product works differently from what buyers expect, a demo helps them understand why it matters.

5. What can your team support?

A demo-led funnel needs people to run demos quickly. A product-led funnel needs strong onboarding, in-app guidance and lifecycle email. Choose the model you can actually operate well.

The hybrid model

Many growing SaaS companies offer both: a self-serve trial for small teams and "Talk to sales" for larger organisations. Product usage then tells sales which trial accounts look like bigger opportunities (many seats invited, heavy usage, company email domains). This is often called product-led sales.

The risk of hybrid is confusion. Make it clear on the pricing page who each path is for, so a small business doesn't wait for a demo and an enterprise buyer doesn't get stuck in a self-serve checkout.

A third option: interactive demos

Between a full trial and a live call sits the interactive demo: a guided, click-through version of the product that visitors can explore without signing up or talking to sales. It shows the key workflow in a few minutes, which suits landing pages and ads for products that need setup before a real trial makes sense (Navattic). It doesn't replace a trial or a sales call, but it can warm visitors up before either.

How the choice changes your marketing

For trial and freemium funnels

  • Paid ads and SEO target problem and tool searches ("free invoice software", "Trello alternative").
  • Landing pages remove friction: short signup, clear first step.
  • Onboarding is marketing: welcome emails, in-app checklists and timely nudges decide conversion.
  • Measure activation, not just signups. Ten thousand signups that never use the product don't help.

The offer in your ads matters too. One analysis of B2B SaaS paid search found that free trial calls to action drew noticeably more clicks than demo offers in non-branded campaigns, though clicks are not the same as revenue (MarTech).

For demo-led funnels

  • Target buyers with intent, including comparison and alternative searches. See our guide to SaaS comparison pages.
  • Qualify on the form, asking company size or use case so sales prioritises well.
  • Respond fast. A demo request that waits a day often goes cold. We cover why in speed to lead.
  • Measure pipeline and revenue, not just demo bookings.

Signs you picked the wrong model

  • Lots of trial signups, few activations: the product needs more guidance, or the trial is too short to reach value.
  • Demos full of tiny accounts: your price or product could support self-serve for this segment.
  • Long sales cycles for low-value deals: sales cost is eating the margin.
  • Enterprise buyers asking for a trial you don't offer: consider a guided pilot.

Conclusion

The free trial vs demo decision comes down to how fast users reach value, what you charge, who buys and what your team can support. Trials and freemium suit simple, lower-priced products bought by individuals and small teams. Demos suit complex, higher-value products bought by groups. A clear hybrid often serves both.

Frequently asked questions

Is a free trial better than a demo for SaaS?

Neither is better in general. A free trial works when users can understand the product and reach value on their own quickly, and the price suits self-serve buying. A demo works better for complex, higher-priced products with several decision-makers. Many companies offer both for different customer sizes.

How long should a SaaS free trial be?

Long enough for a typical user to reach a clear moment of value, and no longer. Simple tools often use 7 or 14 days; products needing setup or team adoption often use 14 or 30. Measure how long activated users take to reach value, then set the trial around that.

Should a free trial require a credit card?

Requiring a card usually means fewer signups but a higher share converting to paid, because only more committed users start. No card usually means more signups and more work on activation. Test both if your volume allows, and judge on paying customers, not signups.

What is the difference between freemium and a free trial?

A free trial gives broad access for a limited time, then requires payment. Freemium offers a permanently free plan with limits, and users pay when they need more. Freemium suits products where free users still create value, such as through referrals or collaboration.

When should a SaaS company add a sales team?

Usually when larger customers start signing up, asking for security reviews, invoices or custom terms, or when the price per account is high enough to justify sales time. Product usage data can show which self-serve accounts are worth a sales conversation.

What is a good trial-to-paid conversion rate?

It varies widely by product, price, whether a card is required, and how users arrive. Rather than chasing a benchmark, track your own rate by channel and improve activation steps. A rising activation rate is usually the clearest sign the trial is working.
