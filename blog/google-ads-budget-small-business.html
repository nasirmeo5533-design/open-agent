How Much Should a Small Business Spend on Google Ads?

By the OpenAgent team

There is no magic number for a Google Ads budget. A plumber in a small town and a law firm in a big city can spend the same amount and get completely different results, because the cost of a click in their markets is completely different. What you can do is work out a budget from your own numbers, so you know what to expect before spending anything.

This guide shows how to work out a Google Ads budget for small business campaigns, what a sensible test period looks like, and how to tell whether to scale up or stop.

Short answer: start from what a customer is worth to you and what clicks cost in your industry. Your budget needs to buy enough clicks to produce several conversions a month, otherwise Google's bidding has nothing to learn from and you can't judge the results. For many small businesses that means a test budget in the range of a few hundred to a couple of thousand dollars a month, run for at least two to three months.

What Google Ads costs a small business

You pay per click on Search campaigns. Google's own guide to how much Google Ads costs explains that there is no fixed price: you set budgets and bids, and the price of a click is set by an auction, so it depends on how many businesses compete for the same searches and how much they are willing to pay.

According to the LocaliQ/WordStream 2025 benchmark report, the average cost per click across industries was about $5.26, up roughly 13% on the previous year, with CPCs rising in most industries. Legal services had the highest average CPC (around $8.58) and arts and entertainment the lowest (around $1.60) (WordStream). Averages hide big differences between specific keywords, so use them as a rough guide only.

To see real estimates for your own keywords, use Google's free Keyword Planner inside a Google Ads account.

How to calculate your Google Ads budget for a small business

Work backwards from the result you want.

  1. Estimate your cost per click (CPC) for your main keywords, using Keyword Planner or the benchmarks above.
  2. Estimate your conversion rate: the share of clicks that become an enquiry, call or sale. If you don't know it, a cautious assumption for a first test is a few percent.
  3. Decide how many conversions you need per month to judge the test. Aim for at least 10 to 15 if you can, more if you want Google's automated bidding to work well.
  4. Multiply: conversions needed ÷ conversion rate × CPC = monthly budget.

A worked example

InputExample value
Average CPC$4
Conversion rate (click to enquiry)5%
Enquiries wanted per month15
Clicks needed15 ÷ 0.05 = 300
Monthly budget300 × $4 = $1,200

Then check this against value. If one in four enquiries becomes a customer, 15 enquiries means about 4 customers. If a customer is worth $1,000 in profit over time, $1,200 of ad spend is easily justified. If a customer is worth $150, the same budget loses money, and you need cheaper keywords, a better conversion rate or a different channel.

What if your budget is small?

A small budget can work if you concentrate it:

  • Target fewer, high-intent keywords. "Emergency boiler repair near me" rather than "boiler".
  • Limit locations and hours to where and when you can actually serve customers.
  • Use one campaign, not five. Spreading a small budget across many campaigns means none gets enough data.
  • Add negative keywords to stop paying for irrelevant searches (jobs, free, DIY, competitors you don't want).

If the maths shows your budget can only buy a handful of clicks a month, Google Ads may not be the right first channel. Local SEO, a Google Business Profile or Meta Ads might be better starting points. We compared those options in Meta Ads vs Google Ads.

A quick check: clicks per day

Some PPC specialists use a simple rule of thumb: if your daily budget can't buy roughly 10 clicks a day on your main keywords, the account will learn very slowly. It is a rough guide, not a rule from Google, but it quickly shows when a campaign is underfunded.

To check yours, divide your daily budget by your expected cost per click. At $30 a day and a $4 CPC, you get about 7 or 8 clicks a day. That might be enough for a very focused local campaign, but too little to test several services at once.

How long to test before judging results

Give a new account at least two to three months. The first weeks are for collecting data: which search terms trigger your ads, which ones convert, and what a realistic cost per lead looks like. Automated bid strategies also need conversion data before they can optimise well.

Judge the test on cost per lead or cost per sale, not on clicks or impressions. A campaign with fewer, more expensive clicks that convert is better than cheap clicks that never call.

Costs beyond ad spend

  • Management: whether you pay an agency or spend your own hours, campaigns need weekly attention: search terms, negatives, bids, ads and landing pages.
  • Landing pages: sending paid clicks to a slow or generic home page wastes budget. A focused page for the service you advertise usually converts better.
  • Tracking: without conversion tracking for calls, forms and WhatsApp clicks, you can't tell which keywords make money.

When to increase or cut your budget

Increase when your cost per lead is below what you can afford and campaigns are limited by budget (Google shows "Limited by budget" in the account). Raise budgets gradually, around 15 to 20% at a time, and watch whether cost per lead holds.

Cut or pause when cost per lead stays above what a customer is worth after a fair test, even after improving keywords, ads and landing pages. Sometimes the right decision is to stop and put the money into another channel.

Conclusion

A Google Ads budget for small business campaigns should come from your own numbers: cost per click in your market, your conversion rate, and what a customer is worth. Budget for enough clicks to learn from, give the test two to three months, and judge it on cost per lead.

Frequently asked questions

What is the minimum budget for Google Ads?

Google doesn't set a meaningful minimum; you can spend a few dollars a day. The practical minimum is the budget that buys enough clicks to produce several conversions a month in your market. If your budget only buys a handful of clicks, you can't learn what works.

Is $500 a month enough for Google Ads?

It can be, in markets with lower click costs and when the budget is focused on a few high-intent keywords and a limited area. In expensive industries like legal or insurance, $500 may buy too few clicks to judge results. Run the calculation with your own CPC and conversion rate first.

How much does a click cost on Google Ads?

It depends on your industry, location and keywords. The LocaliQ/WordStream 2025 benchmarks put the cross-industry average around $5.26 per click, with legal services near the top and arts and entertainment near the bottom. Check Keyword Planner for estimates on your own keywords.

How long does Google Ads take to work?

Ads can bring clicks on the first day, but a campaign usually needs two to three months of data to find which keywords and ads convert profitably. Automated bidding strategies also need a learning period. Judge results on cost per lead after that period, not after the first week.

Should I use daily or monthly budgets?

Google Ads works with an average daily budget, and it can spend up to twice the daily amount on some days while keeping the monthly total within about 30.4 times the daily budget. Set the daily budget from your monthly plan divided by 30.4.

Do I need an agency to run Google Ads?

Not necessarily. Many owners run simple campaigns themselves. An agency or specialist is useful when you don't have time for weekly management, when click costs are high and mistakes are expensive, or when you need proper conversion tracking and landing pages.
